The Breakdown of cancer

What is cancer

Cancer is a disease that happens when some cells in the body begin to grow and divide in an uncontrolled way. Normally, cells grow, divide and die in an organised process. Cancer can develop when this process becomes abnormal. The abnormal cells may form a mass called a tumour. Some tumours are not cancerous, while cancerous tumours can invade nearby tissues and may spread to other parts of the body.

How does cancer develop?

Cancer can develop when changes occur in the cells' instructions, for instance DNA inside a cell becomes changed or damaged., causing them to grow when they should not.

Over time, these abnormal cells may form a tumour. Some tumours are harmless, while cancerous tumours can grow into nearby tissues.

Can cancer spread?

Yes. Some cancers can spread from their original location to other parts of the body.

This process is called metastasis

When cancer spreads, it can affect other organs and make treatment more difficult.

Not all cancers spread, especially when they are found and treated early.

What is breast cancer?

Breast cancer is a type of cancer that starts in the cells of the breast.

It can occur in both women and men, although it is much more common in women.

Breast cancer can affect different parts of the breast and may develop in different ways.

What are the common types of breast cancer?

There are different types of breast cancer. Some common ones include:

Invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC):

A common type of breast cancer that starts in the milk ducts and spreads into nearby breast tissue (Cancer.gov, 2025).

Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC):

A type of breast cancer that starts in the milk-producing glands (lobules) and spreads into nearby tissue (Cancer.gov, 2025).

Inflammatory breast cancer:

A rare and fast-growing type of breast cancer that can make the breast look red, swollen and inflamed (Cancer.gov, 2025).

Triple-negative breast cancer:

A type of breast cancer that does not have three common receptors or proteins that some breast cancers use to grow (Cancer.gov, 2025).

Metastatic breast cancer:

Breast cancer that has spread from the breast to other parts of the body and is also known as Stage 4 breast cancer (Cancer.gov, 2025).

Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS):

An early form of breast cancer that stays inside the milk ducts and has not spread into surrounding breast tissue (Cancer.gov, 2025).

Paget disease of the breast:

A rare type of breast cancer that affects the skin of the nipple and the darker area around it (areola) (Cancer.gov, 2025).

Phyllodes tumour:

A rare breast tumour that develops in the connective tissue, and most phyllodes tumours are not cancerous (Cancer.gov, 2025).

Lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS):

A condition where abnormal cells develop in the milk-producing glands and increase the risk of developing breast cancer in the future (Cancer.gov, 2025).

A healthcare professional can determine the specific type through medical tests.

Can men get breast cancer?

Yes. Men can get breast cancer too.

Although breast cancer is less common in men, they can still develop the disease because men also have a small amount of breast tissue.

Men should therefore not ignore unusual changes in the breast or chest area.

What are the main risk factors?

A risk factor is something that may increase a person's chance of developing a disease.

Some breast cancer risk factors include:

  • Getting older.
  • Family history of breast cancer.
  • Certain inherited genetic changes.
  • Some hormonal factors.
  • Certain lifestyle and environmental factors.

Having a risk factor does not mean that a person will develop cancer.

Why is early detection important?

Finding breast cancer early can give a person a better chance of receiving treatment sooner.

This is why it is important to:

  • Know what is normal for your body.
  • Pay attention to unusual changes.
  • Follow recommended screening advice.
  • Seek professional medical help when concerned.

Early awareness can lead to early action.
